Denser traffic

We kept lanes, right-hand driving and speed limits in the MetaDrive simulator and took out every rule that gives a car priority at a junction.

One car first learns to drive alone in a driving school. We call the result the school graduate. Its policy is copied into every car of a fleet, and the fleet keeps learning together on one scene. The paper asks what these fleets come to agree on and how they settle each encounter. This page shows them doing it.

The clips run the trained fleets, unchanged, in continuous traffic: as many cars as each scene holds, with a new trip starting the moment one ends, so streams keep arriving from every direction. We only added the camera and the 3D models. Every number on the page comes from the paper's analysis outputs, which use the tests at the training volume.

SetupFrom one car to a fleet

All ten methods use one PPO trainer and start from the same driver, so what differs is how they learn in traffic.

ScenesSix more scenes

Each fleet trains and is tested on one scene, most of them built around one kind of conflict.

Every clip shows MAPPO-L in continuous traffic, with 14 to 20 cars at the junctions and the on-ramp (it trained with 8). Hover over a clip to play it. On a phone, clips play as you scroll to them.

PerceptionWhat a car senses

A car never sees pixels. Each step it gets 175 numbers.

The sensor view rides with one car and draws those numbers over a darkened city. Switch views at any time: both show the same moment of the same test.

Section 5.1The graduate already took turns. Training made the turns cleaner.

Two cars are in a conflict when their planned paths cross. The graduate already lets one pass after the other.

What training changes is the gap between them and how often the meeting ends with no contact and no near miss. The same crossing under four fleets:

For six of the seven methods we classify, clean endings rise from the graduate's % to 40–53%, and contact falls from % to 4–13%. Fleets of one method trained apart also mix with little loss: with half the cars from each, arrival moves by −4.5 to +2.7 points (four methods, four scenes).

Section 5.2Who goes first barely moves

First come, first served is in the graduate before any traffic. The right-hand rule never shows up.

Each arrow runs from the graduate's share to a trained method's. For every rule except first come, first served, 50% means the rule plays no role.

The graduate learned alone, yet % of its events at the crossing are first come, first served. Fleets trained from scratch at two scenes reach it too. Right-first shares stay at 45–51% for the four main methods. The one shift, through traffic first under MAPPO-L, points the way the human rule does at unsigned T-junctions, but kinematics or the cost may explain it as well.

Section 5.3Trained cars cut the left turn

At the apex of every left turn we measure how far the car's path lies from the centre line of the lane it should follow.

Pooling the trained methods, the apex lies inside the arc at all four junction scenes, while the graduate's interval includes zero at each. The difference to the graduate is clear at the diamond and in the street grid. The cut is a small fraction of a lane width, and the reward, which pays progress at speed, may explain it as well as kinematics.

Section 5.4Shared networks keep moving. Budgeted independent cars stand still.

The shared networks without reward mixing arrive most often. A per-step safety budget on independent cars is met by not moving.

Trained on no more data than one independent network gets, Shared PPO and MAPPO-L still arrive in 79% of test trips, against 70% for Direct PPO (seeds 1–3). A car that stands accrues almost no cost, and the cost does not charge timeouts.
